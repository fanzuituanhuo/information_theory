\section*{The one idea, and the map}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The one idea, and the map}
\warmth{0}

\begin{strand}
A channel is nothing but a conditional law $p(y\mid x)$. We cannot change that
law, but we may choose how often we use each input. \keyword{Capacity} is the
best mutual information such a choice can buy:
$C=\max_{p(x)}\MI(X;Y)$. Every example in this lecture is one instance of the
same two-step move --- split $\MI(X;Y)=\Ent(Y)-\Ent(Y\mid X)$, then make the
output as uncertain as the channel allows.
\end{strand}

2 & $p(y^n\mid x^n)=\prod_ip(y_i\mid x_i)$ & What does ``memoryless'' rule out?\\
3 & $C=\max_{p(x)}\MI(X;Y)$ & How much information can one channel use carry?\\
4 & $C=1$ (noiseless binary) & What does a perfect bit pipe look like?\\
5 & $\Ent(X\mid Y)=0$ & When does noise cost nothing at all?\\
6 & $C=1-h(p)$ (BSC) & What do flipped bits cost?\\
7 & $C=1-\alpha$ (BEC) & What do lost bits cost?\\
8 & $C=\log|\Y|-\Ent(r)$ & What does symmetry buy us for free?\\
9 & $0\le C\le\log\min(|\X|,|\Y|)$ & Which values of $C$ are possible at all?\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\block{Conventions}
Alphabets $\X$ and $\Y$ are finite. All logarithms are base $2$, so entropies
and capacities are in bits per channel use. We write $h(p)=-p\log
p-(1-p)\log(1-p)$ for the binary entropy function, and $\Ent(r)$ for the entropy
of a probability vector $r$. The input distribution $p(x)$ is ours to choose;
the transition law $p(y\mid x)$ is not.

\section*{Lecture 3 · Channel capacity}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Lecture 3 · Channel capacity}
\begin{strand}
First fix what a channel is and what memorylessness means. Then define capacity
as a maximisation over input distributions, and compute it in the four canonical
examples: the noiseless binary channel, a channel whose outputs give the input
away, the binary symmetric channel, and the binary erasure channel. Finally
isolate the structural reason those computations were easy --- symmetry --- and
record the general properties of $C$.
\end{strand}
