\section*{The one idea, and the map}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The one idea, and the map}
\warmth{0}

\begin{strand}
A probability law tells us which outcomes are likely. Information theory turns
this into \keyword{logarithmic surprise}, then averages, compares, and conditions
it to obtain entropy, divergence, and mutual information. The rest of the
lecture establishes what these quantities can do under comparison and processing,
and ends by turning residual uncertainty into a lower bound on estimation error.
\end{strand}

\block{The map: eight stops from surprise to error bounds}
\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l L@{}}
\toprule
{\color{indigo}stop} & {\color{indigo}idea} & \multicolumn{1}{l}{\color{madder}what it unlocks}\\
\midrule
1 & $-\log p(x)$: surprise
  & turns a probability law into an additive information scale\\
2 & $\Ent(X)$: average surprise
  & quantifies uncertainty and identifies the maximum-entropy law\\
3 & $\KL(p\Vert q)$: model mismatch
  & compares a true law with a reference law, in a directed way\\
4 & $\MI(X;Y)$ and $\Ent(Y\mid X)$
  & separate shared information from uncertainty that remains\\
5 & log-sum and divergence inequalities
  & give non-negativity, chain rules, and convexity\\
6 & conditional mutual information
  & yields data processing: transformations cannot create information\\
7 & entropy under constraints
  & gives range, equality cases, and the effect of deterministic maps or mixing\\
8 & Fano's inequality
  & converts residual uncertainty into a lower bound on decoding error\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\block{Notation used throughout}
Unless a base is shown, $\log=\log_2$ and information is measured in bits.
For zero-probability terms we use $0\log 0=0$.

\begin{strand}
Build the four basic quantities from a probability law: surprise, entropy,
divergence, and mutual information, with conditional entropy as the bridge.
Then use log-sum, non-negativity, and chain rules to show that processing
cannot create information, before turning decoding errors into entropy bounds.
\end{strand}
