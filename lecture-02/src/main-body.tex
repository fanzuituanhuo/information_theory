\section*{The one idea, and the map}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The one idea, and the map}
\warmth{0}

\begin{strand}
A source code replaces each source symbol by a finite string. Its quality has
two sides: \keyword{efficiency}, measured by expected length, and
\keyword{decodability}, tested after codewords are concatenated. Kraft--McMillan
controls which lengths fit; the AEP then shows why entropy is the natural scale
for coding long i.i.d. blocks.
\end{strand}

\block{The map so far}
\noindent\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}c l L@{}}
\toprule
{\color{indigo}stop} & {\color{indigo}object} & \multicolumn{1}{l}{\color{madder}question}\\
\midrule
1 & $C:\X\to\D^*$ & Which finite string represents each source symbol?\\
2 & $L(C)=\E[\ell(X)]$ & How many code symbols are used on average?\\
3 & $C^*:\X^*\to\D^*$ & Can concatenated codewords still be decoded uniquely?\\
4 & $\sum_xD^{-\ell(x)}\le1$ & Which length lists can belong to a decodable code?\\
5 & $-n^{-1}\log p(X^n)\to\Ent(X)$ & Where does the probability mass of a long block concentrate?\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\block{Conventions}
The source alphabet $\X$ is finite, and $\D^*$ is the set of all finite-length
strings over a $D$-ary code alphabet $\D$. Most examples use
$\D=\{0,1\}$.

\section*{Lecture 2 · Source coding}
\phantomsection
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Lecture 2 · Source coding}
\begin{strand}
Assign strings to symbols, average their lengths under the source law, and then
ask the decisive question: does concatenation preserve enough structure to undo
the encoding? For long i.i.d. blocks, identify the typical sequences that carry
almost all probability and reveal entropy as the exponential counting rate.
\end{strand}
